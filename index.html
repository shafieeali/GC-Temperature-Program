The general elution problem

A mixture of compounds with a wide range of boiling points can’t be separated well at one temperature. At a low temperature the early compounds are nicely separated but the late ones take hours and come out as broad, flat peaks; at a high temperature the late ones elute in good time but the early ones crowd together at the dead time. Load the n-alkanes C5–C20 and try both.

Why temperature programming works

Raising the temperature lowers every retention factor, because a larger fraction of each compound is in the gas phase at any moment. In a program, each compound sits almost motionless at the head of the column until the oven is hot enough that its k drops to a few; then it moves off and elutes. Every compound therefore leaves with a similar small k, so all peaks are similarly sharp and evenly spaced. The k vs temperature view shows this: the dots mark where each alkane leaves the column.

Equations

Isothermal: tR = tM(1 + k), σ = tR/√N. Programmed: T(t) = T₁ + r(t − thold), and a compound elutes at the time tR when ∫ dt/[tM(1 + k(T(t)))] = 1, with peak width σ ≈ tM(1 + kelution)/√N. For a homologous series, ln k increases linearly with the number of carbons, and decreases with temperature as ΔH/RT.

A critical pair and the Kovats index

Ethylbenzene and p-xylene elute between octane and nonane on a nonpolar column, with Kovats retention indices of about 858 and 867. They are a real critical pair: a fast ramp or a high temperature merges them, which is the price of a short run. The retention index places any compound on the alkane scale, I = 100[n + (log t′x − log t′n)/(log t′n+1 − log t′n)], and stays nearly constant from instrument to instrument, which is why libraries use it.

Retention is modeled with ln k = (a + b·n)/T + c + d·n, fitted for n-alkanes on a 30 m nonpolar column, with constant dead time. Peaks are Gaussian with equal areas; very sharp peaks are cut off at the top. Real columns also change carrier gas velocity with temperature.
